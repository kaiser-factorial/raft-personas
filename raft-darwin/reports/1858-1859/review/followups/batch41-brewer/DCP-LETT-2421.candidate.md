## Complete surviving body transcription (body only)

The body is one retained body block. Editorial superscript markers are retained in brackets where they occur in the page transcription:

> My Dear Dr In regard to the subject matter of Mr Darwins note to you I have to say that I have never known an instance in which ⟨our⟩ Yellow-billed Cuckoo has been known to drop an egg in the nest of any other bird. I have however seen a nest (—it was exhibited so not found in the field—) of the Chipping Sparrow containing the egg of a Black-billed Cuckoo—but I have always supposed it was deposited there by human [space] [3] [I know] of few birds more faithful apparently to their own young than both species of our cuckoos. Very truly yrs | T M Brewer Dr A. A. Gould

The damaged-page markup includes editorial angle brackets and illegible/blank insertions. The wording establishes Brewer’s personal denial of any known Yellow-billed Cuckoo instance, while the sparrow nest was exhibited and not found in the field; he says he has always supposed the cuckoo egg was placed there by a human. It is therefore evidence of Brewer’s knowledge and judgment, not a field observation establishing cuckoo parasitism.

### Footnotes

1. The letter was previously published in *Correspondence* vol. 7 without the note by CD. Darwin had asked Gould for information on American cuckoo habits; Gould forwarded the queries to Thomas Mayo Brewer, an authority on birds’ eggs, and sent Brewer’s reply to Darwin (see *Correspondence* vol. 7, letter to A. A. Gould, 6 April [1858]).
2. The date is based on *Correspondence* vol. 7, letter to A. A. Gould, 6 April [1858], n. 1.
3. The yellow-billed cuckoo was *Cuculus americanus* (synonym *Coccyzus americanus*); the black-billed cuckoo was *Cuculus erythropthalmus* (synonym *Coccyzus erythropthalmus*); the chipping sparrow was *Fringilla passerina* (synonym *Spizella passerina*).
4. “Ch. 10” refers to chapter 10 of Darwin’s posthumously published “Big book” on species, “Mental powers and instincts of animals,” where Darwin discussed the American cuckoo (*Natural selection*, pp. 506–7).
5. William Yarrell’s *A history of British birds* (Yarrell 1845).
6. Darwin had vol. 1 of John James Audubon’s *Ornithological biography* (Audubon 1831–[9]); he also refers to Alexander Wilson’s *American ornithology* (Wilson 1808–14).
7. In Wilson 1808–14, 4: 14, 16, Wilson said that yellow-billed cuckoo egg size was proportionable to the bird, while black-billed cuckoo eggs were smaller.
8. Louis Agassiz’s *Bibliographia zoologiæ et geologiæ* 3: 111 lists John Edward Gray’s remarks before the Zoological Society of London on 25 October 1836; Gray suggested that the female cuckoo sometimes continued to look after young in another bird’s nest.
